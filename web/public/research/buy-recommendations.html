React Plumber · Recommendations

Four ways to make crafting attention’s buy button answer the press

Each one is built in crafting attention’s own colours, type and corner, grounded in an idea that ships in public button libraries, and checked in a real browser before it reached this page. Press them; they are live.

The moment: a first-time visitor on the pricing page, buying lifetime access. Judged 20 November 2026.

As shipped

Start here

The button already does the right thing for this moment: one press. It only opens checkout, and checkout is where the payment is confirmed, so a slide or a hold here would make people commit twice. The recommendations keep the single press and make the press itself feel answered.

Press and hold to watch it; slide off to drain it

1 · Press, and it pours

The press lands where your finger did

A deeper purple floods out from the exact point you pressed. The words are drawn twice, so they stay readable on both sides of the edge. Sliding off before letting go drains it back, and nothing is bought.

Pick it when the brand wants the press to feel physical without adding a step.

Press it and watch the arrow while checkout opens

2 · Press, and the arrow leads

The wait moves the way you’re going

The button’s own pixel arrow sets off when you press, and while checkout opens it keeps dashing out to the right and back in, a progress mark made of something the button already had.

Pick it when checkout takes a beat to open and the brand’s arrow is worth keeping in view.

Press it: the words roll over

3 · Press, and it says what’s happening

The moment before checkout has a voice

The label rolls up to “Opening checkout”, and a thin line grows along the bottom edge while it does. The words move but never fade, which is the fault that broke the library version of this idea: its label dropped to half strength mid-change.

Pick it when people press twice because nothing seems to happen.

Point at it to see the light pass

4 · A sheen, once

The eye finds it, and it doesn’t nag

A soft band of light crosses the button when it comes into view and again when the pointer arrives. It never loops, it passes under the words, and it doesn’t appear at all for people who ask for reduced motion.

Pick it when the card is busy and the button needs a moment of attention, not a permanent one.

How they were checked: each was pressed to the end, started and abandoned, used from the keyboard alone, and used again with reduced motion turned on, while plumber read the words on it every tenth of a second. The five standards come from Nielsen’s heuristics, Apple’s and Google’s design guidelines and WCAG 2.2. The ideas come from 81 working buttons out of 335 recorded from public libraries.
